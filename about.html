About

The long way to engineering

And why I think it was the right way for me.

How I got here

I didn't start in engineering. My first year at TMU was in Social Work. I went in because I wanted to help people, and that part never changed.

What changed was how I wanted to do it. Over that year, I realized I was more drawn to the behind-the-scenes side of helping: the tools, systems, and services that shape people's experiences long before they walk into an office or a clinic. I started researching where that kind of work happens, and biomedical engineering kept coming up. It gave me a way to build technical skills while keeping the same purpose I started with.

So I switched, and here I am. That year in social work still shapes how I think. I don't only ask whether a technology works. I ask who it works for, and who it might leave out.

Technology and the people it's built for shouldn't be separate questions.

Alongside my degree, I'm working on minors in Philosophy and Biological Psychology. If I'm going to design for people, I want to understand them: how their brains and bodies work, and the ethical questions that come with building things that shape their lives.

Why accessibility and policy

Accessibility is where my interests meet. It's technical, because a product or space either works for someone or it doesn't. It's also about systems, because whether someone can fully take part often depends on policies, processes, and who was considered when those were designed.

A lot of my work at TMU has started with noticing that the information or support existed, but wasn't usable. University accessibility resources can be written in dense policy language that's hard to get through when you're already struggling, which is why I built the Accessibility @ TMU Pocket Guide. Through Grit with Grace, I've also been thinking about how accessibility isn't only for students with permanent disabilities. Grief, injuries, and hard stretches of life affect people's ability to participate too.

That's also why policy interests me. Regulations and institutional decisions quietly decide what's safe, what's available, and who gets considered. Serving on Academic Integrity Council hearing panels has shown me what careful, fair decision-making looks like in practice, and it's the kind of thinking I want to bring to healthcare technology.

Where I'm headed

I'm keeping my options open on purpose. What I know is that I want work that helps people from behind the scenes, making the technology, systems, and decisions around healthcare and accessibility work better for the people who rely on them.

Right now, I'm especially interested in:

  • Medical device regulation and regulatory affairs
  • Quality systems and risk management
  • Accessibility and health policy
  • Technical operations, data, and analytics
  • Clinical and rehabilitation engineering
